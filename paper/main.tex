\begin{abstract}
Noise-robustness claims for convolutional bearing fault classifiers often rest on the Case Western Reserve University (CWRU) data, where load-wise splits test on bearings seen in training. We ask whether a fixed high-pass front end that subtracts an 11-sample moving average makes one-dimensional CNNs (1D-CNNs) more robust once this leakage is controlled. We test it in a standard 1D-CNN, with a learnable variant, and in a dual-stream attention network (PAC-1DCNN), against a WDCNN-style network and random forests on spectral or bearing-kinematic features, under load-wise and defect-size hold-out splits of CWRU and two bearing-disjoint protocols on Paderborn University data. Test signals receive low-pass, white or high-pass synthetic noise at signal-to-noise ratios of 10 to $-5$\,dB; comparisons use paired Wilcoxon tests with Holm correction. A common band limit removes a resampling artefact that otherwise identifies the healthy CWRU class; an interference line that also marks it remains. The front end's effect follows its frequency response: with unseen defect sizes it helps the standard CNN under low-pass noise and hurts it under white and high-pass noise (significant in \res{fs-filter-nsig} of \res{fs-filter-ntests} comparisons), a learnable version behaves alike, and inside PAC-1DCNN it has no significant effect. PAC-1DCNN is never significantly better than the standard CNN. With unseen defect sizes the networks reach \res{fs-mixed-nets-clean-min}--\res{fs-mixed-nets-clean-max}\% clean macro-F1 and the spectral random forest \res{fs-mixed-rf-clean-f1}\%; on Paderborn the networks reach \res{pu-a2r-nets-clean-min}--\res{pu-realcv-nets-clean-max}\%, against \res{pu-chance-a2r-f1}--\res{pu-chance-realcv-f1}\% for random guessing. Kinematic features made the random forest markedly more noise-robust on Paderborn, but not on CWRU. Generalisation across bearings, not noise, limits these models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Rolling-element bearing diagnosis from vibration signals is a long-standing condition-monitoring problem \cite{randall2011tutorial,lei2020review}, and one-dimensional convolutional neural networks (1D-CNNs) trained on raw signal windows have become a common data-driven approach \cite{zhang2017wdcnn,zhang2018ticnn,zhao2020benchmark}. Industrial signals contain background noise, sensor drift and impulsive disturbances that laboratory recordings largely lack, so a recurring design goal is robustness to such impairments. Several architectures pursue it by building signal-processing knowledge into the first layers, for example wide first-layer kernels intended to suppress high-frequency noise \cite{zhang2017wdcnn}, wavelet-parameterised first layers \cite{li2022wkn}, or physics-informed spectral preprocessing combined with attention \cite{kim2023tfmd}.

How such robustness is measured matters as much as how it is designed. The CWRU bearing data \cite{cwrudata} have become a standard reference for testing diagnostic algorithms \cite{smith2015cwru}; in them each seeded fault exists on one physical bearing that was recorded at four motor loads, and all healthy data come from a single bearing \cite{abburi2023closer,rosa2024multilabel}. Random window splits place overlapping segments of one recording in both training and test sets, and splits by motor load still test on bearings seen in training \cite{hendriks2022cwru,abburi2023closer,rosa2024multilabel}. Bearing-wise evaluation across several datasets has shown that common partitions inflate performance and that the number of distinct training bearings is decisive \cite{vieira2026realistic}. Data leakage of this kind is a recognised source of irreproducible results across machine-learning-based science \cite{kapoor2023leakage}.

This paper asks a narrow question that both literatures leave open: \emph{does a fixed, physics-motivated high-pass front end improve the noise robustness of a 1D-CNN bearing classifier once train/test leakage is controlled, and does any gain depend on the spectrum of the noise used to evaluate it?} The front end studied here subtracts an 11-sample moving average from each channel, i.e.\ a fixed high-pass filter, and feeds the residual to one stream of a dual-stream network with attention and post-hoc temperature scaling. We refer to the complete model as PAC-1DCNN. An earlier version of this work reported gains for this model on a single CWRU load split; that evaluation leaked validation data into training and is superseded by the results below.

The contributions are empirical:
\begin{itemize}
\item A controlled comparison of the fixed front end at two model capacities (a standard 1D-CNN with and without it; PAC-1DCNN with and without it), with a learnable variant of the same form, against a WDCNN-style network and random forests on spectral or bearing-kinematic features, under protocols with progressively less train/test overlap: leave-one-load-out and fault-size hold-out on CWRU, and bearing-disjoint protocols on the Paderborn University data \cite{lessmeier2016kat}.
\item A check for class-specific preprocessing artefacts, which shows that resampling only CWRU's 48\,kHz healthy recordings makes them identifiable from one frequency band, and a common band limit that removes this shortcut but not an interference line near 4.2\,kHz that also marks the healthy class.
\item A noise-spectrum sweep (low-pass, white and high-pass noise at 10 to $-5$\,dB SNR, plus drift and impulses) with two training regimes, which exposes a confound between the spectrum of the synthetic noise and the passband of the front end.
\item Paired, multiplicity-corrected statistics over folds and seeds, reporting effect sizes and win counts rather than single-run accuracies, and a code base in which every number in this paper is generated from saved results.
\end{itemize}
We do not claim a new architecture. The results are largely negative for PAC-1DCNN, and the front end helps a standard CNN only when the test noise lies mostly below its cut-off; we report both as found.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Noise-robust CNNs for bearing diagnosis}
WDCNN uses wide kernels in the first convolutional layer to extract features and suppress high-frequency noise, and adaptive batch normalisation for domain adaptation across loads \cite{zhang2017wdcnn}; the same group studied training methods for CNN diagnosis under noisy environments and different working loads \cite{zhang2018ticnn}. WaveletKernelNet replaces the first convolutional layer with a continuous-wavelet layer whose scale and translation are learned \cite{li2022wkn}. Kim and Kim combine time- and frequency-domain 1D-CNN branches, physics-informed spectral preprocessing and a channel-spatial attention module for noise robustness \cite{kim2023tfmd}. Channel and spatial attention of this kind follows squeeze-and-excitation \cite{hu2018se} and CBAM \cite{woo2018cbam}. Zhao et al.\ benchmarked MLP, auto-encoder, CNN and RNN models on publicly available datasets with two data-split strategies and released a common code base \cite{zhao2020benchmark}. Robustness in such studies is usually assessed by adding synthetic noise at controlled signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) to the test signals, e.g.\ from $-6$ to 6\,dB in \cite{kim2023tfmd}. In the work we examined we did not find an analysis of how the outcome depends on the spectrum of that noise relative to the pass band of the network's front end; our search was not exhaustive.

\subsection{Evaluation leakage in bearing datasets}
Smith and Randall applied three established diagnostic techniques to the entire CWRU data set, categorised the records by how clearly they could be diagnosed, and found the outcome to depend more on the rig assembly, which changed with each installed bearing, than on fault size or load \cite{smith2015cwru}. Hendriks et al.\ pointed out that splits by operating condition reuse the same faulted bearings in training and test sets, proposed dividing the data by fault size so that they do not, and found the accuracy of several CNNs to drop by about 45\% on average under that division \cite{hendriks2022cwru}; Abburi et al.\ reported that assigning data from one bearing to both training and evaluation gives over-optimistic performance estimates \cite{abburi2023closer}. Rosa et al.\ noted that, because CWRU contains a single healthy bearing pair, any division of the healthy class leaks, and proposed a multi-label formulation in which whole fault conditions, and all healthy recordings, are held out for testing; they also note that the healthy recordings are available only at 48\,kHz \cite{rosa2024multilabel}. Vieira et al.\ proposed bearing-wise partitioning across CWRU, Paderborn, UORED-VAFCLS and HUST data and found the number of distinct training bearings decisive \cite{vieira2026realistic}. The Paderborn data contain experiments with 32 bearings (6 healthy, 12 with artificial damage and 14 with damage from accelerated lifetime tests) and come with bearing-disjoint training and test sets for transfer from artificial to real damage and for cross-validation over real damage \cite{lessmeier2016kat}. Our study applies these evaluation principles to the specific question of front-end design for noise robustness.

\subsection{Calibration under shift}
Temperature scaling divides the logits by a scalar fitted on held-out data and is effective for in-distribution calibration \cite{guo2017calibration}; under dataset shift, post-hoc calibration of this kind was found to fall short \cite{ovadia2019shift}. PAC-1DCNN includes temperature scaling as a component; we test whether it helps when the test noise differs from the calibration data.

\section{Problem Setting and Evaluation Protocols}
\subsection{Task}
A window $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times L}$ of $L$ samples from $C$ accelerometer channels, sampled at $f_s$, is to be assigned a class $y\in\{1,\dots,K\}$. On CWRU, $C=2$ (drive end and fan end), $f_s=12$\,kHz, $L=1024$ and $K=4$ (normal, inner race, ball, outer race). On Paderborn, $C=1$, $f_s=16$\,kHz after decimation, $L=2048$ and $K=3$ (healthy, inner ring, outer ring). Each channel of each window is standardised to zero mean and unit variance, using that window's own statistics, before any impairment is added; no normalisation statistics are estimated across windows.

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}
\emph{CWRU.} We use \res{cwru-recordings} recordings \cite{cwrudata}: the 12\,kHz drive-end recordings of inner-race, ball and outer-race (centred, 6~o'clock) faults of 0.007, 0.014 and 0.021\,in at loads of 0--3\,hp, and the four normal-baseline recordings. The normal-baseline recordings are available only at 48\,kHz \cite{smith2015cwru,rosa2024multilabel}; read at 12\,kHz, their shaft-speed line appears at a quarter of the rotation frequency. We therefore decimate them by four with the anti-aliasing filter used for the Paderborn data below. Decimation alone would create a second artefact: its filter rolls off above 4.8\,kHz, whereas the 12\,kHz fault recordings retain content up to 6\,kHz, as well as the quantisation steps of their analogue-to-digital conversion. Every CWRU recording is therefore low-passed to a common band limit of \res{cwru-bandlimit-hz}\,Hz (eighth-order Chebyshev type~I, applied forwards and backwards). We checked the result with two artefact features, the share of window power between 5.2 and 6\,kHz and the share of distinct sample values (\texttt{cwru\_shortcut\_check.py}). Without the band limit they separate healthy from faulty windows with an area under the ROC curve of up to \res{shortcut-before-edgeclean} and \res{shortcut-before-distinctvalues} (taking $\max(\mathrm{AUC},1-\mathrm{AUC})$ over the four loads); with it, the 5.2--6\,kHz band lies at about $\res{shortcut-after-edgedb-healthy}$\,dB, the numerical floor, in both classes, and the values are at most \res{shortcut-after-edgeclean} and \res{shortcut-after-distinctvalues}. Earlier versions of our pipeline omitted both steps; all results here use the corrected data. Windows are cut with a stride of 256 samples, giving \res{cwru-windows} windows.

Two properties of the recordings themselves remain. First, Smith and Randall attribute discrete components at harmonics of about 4.2\,kHz in the CWRU data to electromagnetic interference from the dynamometer controller \cite{smith2015cwru}. The first harmonic lies just above our band limit, where the filter attenuates it by only a few decibels. The 4.0--4.4\,kHz band has a similar absolute level in both classes (\res{shortcut-after-line-absdb-min}--\res{shortcut-after-line-absdb-max}\,dB higher in healthy windows), but because the healthy vibration is weak it holds \res{shortcut-after-line-healthy-pct-min}--\res{shortcut-after-line-healthy-pct-max}\% of the power of a standardised healthy window (median per load) and at most \res{shortcut-after-line-faulty-pct-max}\% of a faulty one, and it separates the classes (separability \res{shortcut-after-line-sep-min} at every load, also under the 15\,dB training noise). Models can thus recognise the healthy class from a component unrelated to bearing condition; we did not remove it (Section~\ref{sec:threats}). Second, using the drive-end signal, Smith and Randall could not, or only partially, diagnose 12 of the 36 fault recordings used here with any of their three envelope-based methods (ball faults 118--120, 185--188, 224 and 225; 0.014-in outer-race faults 198--200), and recordings 236 and 237 contain clipped sections \cite[Tables~3 and~B2]{smith2015cwru}.

\emph{Paderborn University (PU).} We use the vibration signal (acceleration of the bearing housing, sampled at 64\,kHz) of the \res{pu-bearings} bearings of type 6203 that do not have damage on both rings, i.e.\ all except KB23, KB24 and KB27 \cite{lessmeier2016kat,kat2016data}. Each 4-s measurement at 64\,kHz is decimated by four with an eighth-order Chebyshev type~I anti-aliasing filter applied forwards and backwards. As in the artificial-to-real experiment of \cite{lessmeier2016kat}, only operating setting N15\_M07\_F10 (1500\,rpm, 0.7\,N\,m, 1000\,N; 20 measurements per bearing) is used, here for both protocols. Training windows overlap by half; test windows do not overlap. Measurements of one bearing are cropped to the shortest among them (at most about 2\% of samples, bearing KA05); one file of an unused bearing and setting (N15\_M01\_F10\_KA08\_2) could not be read.

\subsection{Protocols}
Table~\ref{tab:protocols} lists the four protocols and what each allows training and test sets to share. P1 holds out one motor load at a time; the load mainly lowers the shaft speed, by up to about 4\%, and puts no radial load on the bearings \cite{smith2015cwru,hendriks2022cwru}, so P1 is a weak shift even apart from the shared bearings. P2 holds out one defect size (all loads) per fold, so the faulty test bearings are never seen in training; it is a variant of the fault-size division of Hendriks et al., who train on one defect size and test on another \cite{hendriks2022cwru}. The single healthy bearing is split in time (first 80\% of each recording for training, last 20\% for testing, 1\% gap), a residual leak we cannot remove on CWRU. P3 and P4 are the artificial-to-real and real-damage cross-validation protocols of \cite{lessmeier2016kat}, in which every test bearing, including healthy ones, is a separate specimen. P4 uses all $\binom{5}{3}=10$ ways of training on three bearings per class and testing on the other two. P3 has a single split, so its statistics reflect only training randomness (initialisation, data order and augmentation) over 20 seeds.

\section{Models}
\subsection{Fixed residual front end}
For each channel, the front end computes
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}_c[n]=\mathbf{x}_c[n]-\frac{1}{11}\sum_{k=-5}^{5}\mathbf{x}_c[n+k],
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
with zero padding at the window edges. Its frequency response is
\begin{equation}
H_r(\omega)=1-\frac{\sin(11\omega/2)}{11\sin(\omega/2)},\qquad \omega=2\pi f/f_s ,
\label{eq:hr}
\end{equation}
a high-pass filter whose amplitude response first reaches 0.5 at about \res{filter-halfamp-hz}\,Hz for $f_s=12$\,kHz (\res{filter-halfamp-hz-pu}\,Hz at 16\,kHz); we refer to this frequency as its cut-off. Above it the response is not flat: the side lobes of the moving average make $|H_r|$ reach \res{filter-maxgain} near \res{filter-maxgain-hz}\,Hz (at 12\,kHz). It has no trainable parameters. It removes slow baseline drift by construction, except within five samples of the window edges where the zero padding biases the moving average, but it equally removes any noise and signal content below its cut-off.

\subsection{PAC-1DCNN}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the network. Stream~R applies two strided convolutions with kernel length 5 (32 and 64 channels) to $\mathbf{r}$; stream~X applies two with kernel length 15 to $\mathbf{x}$; each convolution is followed by batch normalisation and ReLU. The concatenated 128 feature maps are fused by a strided convolution of length 3, giving $\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times L/8}$. The attention block computes feature-channel weights $\mathbf{a}=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_2\,\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_1\,\mathrm{GAP}(\mathbf{Z})))$ in the manner of squeeze-and-excitation \cite{hu2018se}, then temporal weights $\boldsymbol{\tau}=\sigma(\mathrm{conv}_7(\mathbf{a}\odot\mathbf{Z}))\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times L/8}$ from a length-7 convolution over all 128 weighted channels, a simplified variant of the spatial attention of CBAM \cite{woo2018cbam}, which convolves channel-pooled maps, and outputs $\boldsymbol{\tau}\odot\mathbf{a}\odot\mathbf{Z}$. Global average pooling and a linear layer give logits $\mathbf{z}$, and $\hat{\mathbf{p}}=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z}/T)$. Note that $\mathbf{a}$ weights the 128 learned feature channels, not the physical sensors, so the attention weights cannot be read as sensor attributions; we did not evaluate them as explanations.

\subsection{Baselines}
\emph{Standard 1D-CNN (CNN):} three strided convolutions (kernel lengths 15, 7, 5; 32, 64, 128 channels) with batch normalisation and ReLU, global average pooling and a linear layer. \emph{CNN+HP:} the same network with the fixed front end \eqref{eq:residual} applied to its input. Together with PAC-1DCNN and PAC-1DCNN without the front end (PAC$-$HP), this gives a $2\times2$ design that separates the front end from model capacity. \emph{WDCNN-style CNN (WDCNN-s):} the layers of Table~2 of \cite{zhang2017wdcnn}: a first convolution of length 64 and stride 16 with 16 kernels, four length-3 convolutions with 32, 64, 64 and 64 kernels (the last without padding), each followed by batch normalisation, ReLU and max-pooling, and a flattened feature map feeding one hidden layer of 100 units with batch normalisation. It differs from the original in its input length on CWRU (1024 instead of 2048 samples), in the number of classes, and in omitting AdaBN, which re-estimates batch-normalisation statistics on the test domain. \emph{Envelope-spectrum random forest (Env-RF):} for each channel, $\log(1+\cdot)$ of the mean Hann-windowed spectral magnitude in 64 equal bands up to the Nyquist frequency, and $\log(1+\cdot)$ of the magnitude of the envelope spectrum (Hilbert envelope, mean removed) up to about 610\,Hz \cite{randall2011tutorial}. These fixed per-window transforms involve no fitted preprocessing; the features are classified by a random forest of 300 trees with balanced class weights \cite{breiman2001rf}.

Three further models address questions raised by the primary results and are analysed as a separate, secondary family (Section~\ref{sec:stats}). \emph{CNN+LHP:} CNN+HP with the 11 moving-average taps of \eqref{eq:residual} made trainable and initialised as in the fixed filter, i.e.\ a learned FIR front end of the same form. \emph{PAC$-$dual:} the single-stream ablation of PAC-1DCNN (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}), which sees the input only through the front end. \emph{Kinematic random forest (Kin-RF):} features restricted to the frequencies that bearing kinematics predicts for each fault. Each channel is band-passed from $f_s/12$ to $f_s/3$ (a fixed, untuned band), its Hilbert envelope spectrum is computed, and for the first three harmonics of the inner-ring, outer-ring and rolling-element fault frequencies the largest envelope magnitude within $\pm3\%$ of the expected frequency (slip commonly shifts it by 1--2\% \cite{smith2015cwru}) is divided by the median envelope magnitude \cite{randall2011tutorial}. The fault frequencies are multiples of the shaft speed: 5.4152, 3.5848 and 4.7135 for the CWRU drive-end bearing as published by the data provider \cite{cwrudata} (the last is twice the ball spin frequency of 2.357 in \cite[Table~2]{smith2015cwru}), and, for the Paderborn bearings, 4.946, 3.054 and 3.993 computed from the 6203 geometry given in \cite[Table~1]{lessmeier2016kat} (for an SKF 6203, \cite[Table~2]{smith2015cwru} lists 4.947, 3.053 and $2\times1.994$). The shaft speed is the value stored with each CWRU recording (for the two healthy recordings that lack it, the nominal 1772 and 1750\,rpm \cite[Table~A1]{smith2015cwru}) and 1500\,rpm on Paderborn. Kin-RF uses the same random forest and training data as Env-RF. Table~\ref{tab:models} gives parameter counts.

\section{Impairment Model and Training}
\subsection{Impairments}
A clean standardised window $\mathbf{x}$ is impaired as $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{e}$. The noise is $\mathbf{n}=\alpha\,(g_s*\mathbf{w})$ with $\mathbf{w}$ white Gaussian, ``same''-length convolution with a shaping kernel $g_s$, and $\alpha$ chosen so that $10\log_{10}(P_x/P_n)$ equals the target SNR, where $P_x$ and $P_n$ are mean powers over all channels and samples of the window. Three spectra are used: \emph{low-pass}, $g[k]=e^{-3k/14}$ for $k=0,\dots,14$ (\res{noise-lowpass-below1k}\% of its power below 1\,kHz at 12\,kHz); \emph{white}, $g=\delta$; and \emph{high-pass}, $g=[-0.5,\,1,\,-0.5]$. With probability $p_d$ a drift $d[n]=a\sin(2\pi c\,n/(L-1))+b\,n/(L-1)$ with $a\sim\mathcal{U}(0.3,0.8)$, $c\sim\mathcal{U}(0.5,2)$, $b\sim\mathcal{U}(-0.5,0.5)$ is added to all channels; with probability $p_e$, two to five impulses of amplitude $\pm\mathcal{U}(2,4)$ and three samples' width are added. Fig.~\ref{fig:spectra} compares the noise spectra with $|H_r|^2$: the low-pass noise lies largely in the stop band of the front end, the high-pass noise in its pass band.

\subsection{Training and test conditions}
All networks are trained with AdamW \cite{loshchilov2019adamw} (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$, batch 64, 15 epochs), class-weighted cross-entropy, and the weights of the final epoch (no model selection on test-like data). Impairments are drawn afresh for every training batch at 15\,dB SNR with $p_d=0.4$, $p_e=0.1$, either with low-pass noise only (\emph{low-pass regime}) or with the spectrum drawn per window from the three types (\emph{mixed regime}); P1 uses the low-pass regime only. The random forests (Env-RF, Kin-RF) are trained on two impaired copies of the training windows under the same regime. Test sets are impaired once with fixed seeds, identically for all models: P1 uses low-pass noise at each SNR together with drift and impulses ($p_d=0.85$, $p_e=0.35$); P2--P4 use each noise spectrum alone at 10, 5, 0 and $-5$\,dB, drift and impulses without noise ($p_d=0.85$, $p_e=0.35$), and clean data. In P1 the temperature $T$ is fitted by minimising the negative log-likelihood on the last 20\% (in time) of each training recording, impaired at 10\,dB with $p_d=0.6$, $p_e=0.2$; in P2--P4 uncalibrated probabilities are reported. Algorithm~\ref{alg:protocol} summarises one fold.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One fold of the evaluation}
\label{alg:protocol}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require bearing-level split $(\mathcal{B}_{\text{tr}},\mathcal{B}_{\text{te}})$, seeds $S$, regimes $R$, test conditions $Q$
\State $X_{\text{tr}},X_{\text{te}}\gets$ standardised windows of $\mathcal{B}_{\text{tr}}$, $\mathcal{B}_{\text{te}}$
\For{$r\in R$, $s\in S$}
  \For{each network $m$}
    \State initialise $m$ with seed $s$
    \For{15 epochs, each mini-batch $B\subset X_{\text{tr}}$}
      \State $\tilde B\gets$ impair$(B;\,r,\,15\,\text{dB})$ with fresh randomness; update $m$ on $\tilde B$
    \EndFor
    \State P1 only: fit $T$ on impaired calibration windows (last 20\% of each training recording)
  \EndFor
  \State P2--P4: fit Env-RF and Kin-RF (seed $s$) on two copies of $X_{\text{tr}}$ impaired under $r$
\EndFor
\For{$q\in Q$}
  \State $\tilde X_{\text{te}}\gets$ impair$(X_{\text{te}};\,q)$ with a seed fixed per (fold, $q$)
  \State evaluate every trained model on $\tilde X_{\text{te}}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}
\label{sec:stats}
We report macro-F1 over windows as the primary metric because the class sizes differ, together with the false-alarm rate (healthy windows predicted as faulty), the missed-fault rate, the expected calibration error (ECE, ten equal-width confidence bins) \cite{guo2017calibration} and, on Paderborn, accuracy per 4-s measurement obtained by averaging window probabilities, which matches the unit used in \cite{lessmeier2016kat}. Models are compared by two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests \cite{wilcoxon1945} over paired (fold, seed) runs, with Holm's procedure \cite{holm1979} applied over all contrasts and test conditions of a family within a protocol and training regime. The primary family holds the five contrasts of the original design (front end at two capacities, PAC-1DCNN against CNN, WDCNN-s and Env-RF; 70 tests); a secondary family holds the three contrasts of the added models (CNN+LHP against CNN+HP, PAC$-$dual against PAC-1DCNN, Kin-RF against Env-RF; 42 tests), so that adding models does not change the primary analysis. With 15 pairs (P2) the smallest attainable two-sided $p$-value of the exact test is about $6\times10^{-5}$, with 20 pairs (P1, P3, P4) about $2\times10^{-6}$, so only effects that are nearly unanimous across runs can be declared significant; we therefore also report mean differences and win counts. The pairs are not independent: runs with different seeds share the test data of their fold, and cross-validation folds share training data. The nominal error rates of such tests are then not guaranteed and $p$-values can be optimistic \cite{dietterich1998,nadeau2003}. We therefore also average each model over seeds within a fold and report how many folds agree in sign with the mean difference, and for the ten folds of P4 we apply the corrected resampled $t$-test of \cite{nadeau2003} to the fold means, with the test-to-training ratio counted in bearings (6/9) and Holm's procedure within each family (\texttt{fold\_level\_stats.py}).

\subsection{P3 and P4: bearing-disjoint protocols (Paderborn)}
\emph{Pipeline check.} Because all models score low on these protocols, we first checked preprocessing, labels and features with the envelope-spectrum random forest trained on clean data. When the windows of the 15 real-damage bearings are split at random, so that every test bearing also contributes training windows, it reaches \res{pu-sanity-leaky-macrof1mean}\% macro-F1 and \res{pu-sanity-leaky-measurementaccuracymean}\% measurement accuracy (five repetitions). On the ten bearing-disjoint P4 folds the same classifier reaches \res{pu-sanity-disjoint-macrof1mean}\% macro-F1 (range \res{pu-sanity-disjoint-macrof1min}--\res{pu-sanity-disjoint-macrof1max}\%) and \res{pu-sanity-disjoint-measurementaccuracymean}\% measurement accuracy. The signals thus identify individual bearings reliably, but the fault type learned from some bearings does not transfer to others. Errors are largely bearing-wise: in \res{pu-sanity-dominant} of the \res{pu-sanity-cases} (fold, test bearing) cases at least 90\% of a bearing's windows receive the same label, and in \res{pu-sanity-dominant-wrong} of these that label is wrong. In the first fold, for example, \res{pu-sanity-train123-KA22-share}\% of the windows of outer-ring bearing KA22 are labelled \res{pu-sanity-train123-KA22-pred} and \res{pu-sanity-train123-KA30-share}\% of those of outer-ring bearing KA30 \res{pu-sanity-train123-KA30-pred}.

\emph{Networks and noise.} On clean data the networks reach \res{pu-realcv-nets-clean-min}--\res{pu-realcv-nets-clean-max}\% macro-F1 in P4 (Table~\ref{tab:pu}, both regimes), where uniform random guessing gives \res{pu-chance-realcv-f1}\%, and \res{pu-a2r-nets-clean-min}--\res{pu-a2r-nets-clean-max}\% in P3, where it gives \res{pu-chance-a2r-f1}\% because the single healthy test bearing makes the classes unbalanced. The random forests reach \res{pu-realcv-mixed-rf-clean-f1}\% and \res{pu-a2r-mixed-rf-clean-f1}\% (Env-RF) and \res{pu-realcv-mixed-kin-clean-f1}\% and \res{pu-a2r-mixed-kin-clean-f1}\% (Kin-RF; mixed regime). In P4 the standard deviation over runs of every network on clean data is more than twice the largest clean difference between networks. Strong broadband noise pushes most models to one side of the healthy/faulty decision, and which side depends on the training bearings: under white noise at 0\,dB (mixed regime) PAC-1DCNN misses \res{pu-realcv-mixed-physics-white0dB-miss}\% of faulty windows in P4 while raising \res{pu-realcv-mixed-physics-white0dB-far}\% false alarms, whereas in P3 it flags \res{pu-a2r-mixed-physics-white0dB-far}\% of healthy windows as faulty. PAC-1DCNN does not differ significantly from the standard CNN in any of the \res{pu-realcv-physics-baseline-ntests} P4 comparisons. In P3 it is ahead on clean data by \res{pu-a2r-mixed-physics-baseline-clean-absdelta} points in the mixed regime (better in \res{pu-a2r-mixed-physics-baseline-clean-wins} runs, not significant) and significantly behind in \res{pu-a2r-physics-baseline-nsig} of \res{pu-a2r-physics-baseline-ntests} conditions (high-pass noise at 0\,dB, mixed regime, \res{pu-a2r-mixed-physics-baseline-highpass0dB-delta} points). \section{Discussion}
\emph{What the results establish.} On CWRU, a protocol that shares physical bearings between training and test makes all models look nearly perfect on clean data. Under strong noise it spreads the architectures widely (\res{lolo--5dB-f1-min} to \res{lolo--5dB-f1-max}\% macro-F1 at $-5$\,dB), but the significant differences involve the single-stream variant, not PAC-1DCNN against the standard CNN. Once defect sizes or bearings are held out, all models, including two classical classifiers, largely fail to identify the fault type, and PAC-1DCNN does not differ significantly from the standard CNN. The fixed front end has significant effects only where its frequency response predicts them: it improves the standard CNN under low-pass noise, whose power lies mostly below its cut-off, and lowers its macro-F1 under white and high-pass noise. A learnable front end of the same form behaves like the fixed one. Inside PAC-1DCNN the raw-signal stream bypasses the front end and removing it has no effect, whereas a network that sees the input only through the front end (PAC$-$dual) inherits its spectrum dependence: better under low-pass noise, worse under high-pass noise. Whether such a front end confers robustness therefore depends on how the noise spectrum relates to the filter, and a robustness claim made with one noise model does not carry over to another. The training augmentation and the original test noise of this work were both low-pass, the configuration most favourable to the front end.

\emph{What they do not establish.} The absence of significant effects in most comparisons is not evidence of no effect: the strict family-wise correction gives low power, and P2 has only three test bearings per fault type. Conversely, the significant effects rest on dependent runs (Section~\ref{sec:stats}). Synthetic noise models of the kind used here, and in much of the literature, are not measurements of plant noise. The collapse of fault-type recall on CWRU may partly reflect the dataset itself: a third of the fault recordings used here are not clearly diagnosable even with envelope analysis \cite{smith2015cwru}.

\emph{Implications for evaluation.} Robustness claims for fixed or learned front ends should be tested with noise whose spectrum overlaps the front end's pass band, not only with the noise used for training, and with splits that hold out physical bearings \cite{vieira2026realistic}. Preprocessing applied to one class only is a further source of shortcuts: in an earlier version of our pipeline, only the healthy CWRU recordings passed through a decimation filter, which made them recognisable from a band that the fault recordings retain, and that version showed a significant P1 advantage of PAC-1DCNN at $-5$ and $-10$\,dB that disappeared once all recordings shared one band limit. Artefact checks of the kind in Section~\ref{sec:data} are cheap and should precede model comparisons. Reporting win counts, fold-level agreement and corrected paired tests makes it visible when an apparent improvement is within run-to-run variation.

\emph{Paderborn.} The Paderborn results are consistent with those on CWRU and sharpen them. Holding out whole bearings leaves every network at most about ten points above random guessing on clean data, while an envelope-spectrum classifier separates the same recordings almost perfectly when bearings are shared; the models therefore learn signatures of individual bearings rather than fault-type features that transfer. With so little transferable information, noise robustness is a secondary question: the fixed front end had no significant effect in any of \res{pu-filter-ntests} Paderborn comparisons, and the highest clean scores came from the random forests. The one physics prior that helped in this study was a prior on features rather than a filter: restricting the random forest to the kinematic fault frequencies gave it the highest mean macro-F1 of all models in every test condition at 0 and $-5$\,dB, in both Paderborn protocols and both training regimes. It did not help on CWRU's fault-size split, so we do not read it as a general remedy; plausible differences are that Paderborn holds out whole bearings with real damage, whereas CWRU holds out defect sizes of seeded faults, and that the fault frequencies are not clearly visible in the envelope spectra of a third of the CWRU fault recordings used here (Section~\ref{sec:data}), but our experiments do not test this. These results come from one pipeline (0.128-s windows at 16\,kHz, one untuned training recipe) that is far below the published measurement-level accuracy in P4, so they describe the limits of this kind of window-level model under bearing-disjoint evaluation, not an upper bound on what the data allow.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\emph{Internal.} All networks share one training recipe (15 epochs, no early stopping, no per-model tuning); a model that needs a different recipe may be disadvantaged. A \res{curves-epochs}-epoch run on one fold per data set (\texttt{training\_curves.py}) shows the training loss at most \res{curves-loss15-max} for every network at epoch 15; test macro-F1 at epoch \res{curves-epochs} was lower than at epoch 15 in \res{curves-nlast-lower} of \res{curves-nruns} cases (differences from $\res{curves-dlast-min}$ to $\res{curves-dlast-max}$ points), so longer training would not change the comparison systematically, although scores at any single epoch vary by several points. The WDCNN-style network follows the layers of \cite{zhang2017wdcnn} but not its input length on CWRU or its test-time AdaBN. The random forest uses a fixed, untuned feature set. \emph{Construct.} Macro-F1 over windows weights each class equally but treats overlapping windows of one recording as separate samples. The CWRU healthy recordings were decimated from 48\,kHz by us, whereas the fault recordings are provided at 12\,kHz; the common band limit removes the band in which the anti-aliasing filters differ (Section~\ref{sec:data}), but other differences between the two acquisitions below \res{cwru-bandlimit-hz}\,Hz cannot be excluded. The interference line near 4.2\,kHz, which the band limit attenuates only partly, marks the healthy class (Section~\ref{sec:data}); it may contribute to the high healthy-class recall and the absence of false alarms on CWRU, although it cannot explain confusions between fault types. A band limit with its stop band below 4\,kHz would remove it; we have not repeated the CWRU experiments with one. Because diagnosability on CWRU depends on the rig assembly, which changed with each installed bearing \cite{smith2015cwru}, an unseen defect size in P2 is also an unseen assembly. Synthetic noise is added after per-window standardisation, so SNR is defined per window. \emph{External.} CWRU contains one bearing per fault condition and one healthy bearing; the Paderborn protocols use 18 (P3) and 15 (P4) bearings of a single type at one operating setting, and P3 has one healthy bearing each for training and testing. On Paderborn we decimate to 16\,kHz and classify 0.128-s windows, so information above 8\,kHz or spread over a whole measurement is not available to the models; this may explain part of the gap to \cite{lessmeier2016kat}. Neither data set contains field data from industrial machines. \emph{Conclusion validity.} Holm correction over 24 (P1), 70 (primary, P2--P4) and 42 (secondary) tests per family limits power, while the dependence between paired runs can make individual $p$-values optimistic; at fold level, no P4 contrast survives the corrected resampled $t$-test. Uncorrected $p$-values, mean differences, win counts and fold agreement are reported to show the size and consistency of the observed effects.

\section{Conclusion}
A fixed residual high-pass front end did not make 1D-CNN bearing fault classifiers robust to noise in general. Once faulty test bearings were held out of training, its effect on CWRU followed its frequency response: it helped a standard CNN under low-pass noise and hurt it under white and high-pass noise, a learnable front end of the same form behaved alike, and inside PAC-1DCNN it had no significant effect; on Paderborn it had none. PAC-1DCNN was never significantly better than the standard CNN, including on the leave-one-load-out split once a resampling artefact in the healthy CWRU class was removed. A WDCNN-style network and an envelope-spectrum random forest performed as well or better, and all models mostly failed to identify race-fault types at unseen defect sizes. On Paderborn the networks scored at most about ten points above random guessing although the same recordings are separated almost perfectly when bearings are shared, and random forests had the highest clean scores; restricting the forest to kinematic fault frequencies made it markedly more noise-robust there, but not on CWRU. Generalisation across bearings, rather than noise, limits these models. Temperature scaling fitted on nearly error-free calibration data made calibration worse under strong noise.
Future work should repeat the CWRU comparison with a band limit below the 4.2\,kHz interference line, and evaluate front ends with field noise recordings and on data sets with many bearings per class.
